\section*{\centering Abstract}

This report presents an intelligent Information Retrieval (IR) system developed for the categorized BBC News Archive (2,225 articles across five topics: Business, Entertainment, Politics, Sport, and Tech). Traditional search techniques like TF-IDF and BM25 are fast and excel at exact keyword matching, but they suffer from the \textit{vocabulary mismatch problem} when users search using synonyms. On the other hand, modern neural models like Sentence Transformers understand deep semantic meaning and context, but can sometimes overlook specific keyword constraints.

To leverage the strengths of both approaches, we designed a \textbf{Hybrid Search Pipeline} that combines probabilistic Okapi BM25 with dense Sentence Transformers (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) using \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}. RRF merges search results based on ranking positions rather than raw scores, avoiding the challenge of calibrating different score ranges.

Additionally, the system includes practical search enhancements:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{WordNet Query Expansion:} Automatically enriches queries with synonyms to reduce vocabulary mismatch.
    \item \textbf{Query-Focused Extractive Summarization:} Generates Google-style featured snippets by highlighting the most relevant sentences in retrieved articles.
    \item \textbf{3D Semantic Knowledge Map:} Uses Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to visualize document clusters and queries in an interactive 3D space.
    \item \textbf{Live News Categorization:} Uses a trained Logistic Regression model (96.63\% accuracy) to categorize and index breaking news articles in real time.
\end{itemize}

Empirical evaluations show that the Hybrid RRF model achieves superior performance with a \textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP) of 0.912} and an \textbf{nDCG@10 of 0.965}, clearly outperforming standalone keyword and semantic search models.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\textbf{Keywords:} Information Retrieval, Hybrid Search, Okapi BM25, Sentence Transformers, Reciprocal Rank Fusion, BBC News, Query Expansion, Extractive Summaries.

\section{Introduction}

Information Retrieval (IR) is the foundational technology that powers search engines, digital news portals, customer support knowledge bases, and modern question-answering systems. At its core, an IR system takes an unstructured natural language query and searches across a large repository of documents to identify, rank, and return the most relevant information.

With the growth of online journalism and digital news media, news archives present unique and demanding search challenges. Users searching a news repository exhibit diverse search intents: some search for specific named entities (e.g., \textit{``Apple fiscal earnings report''}), while others submit broad conceptual questions (e.g., \textit{``impact of inflation on consumer spending''}). A successful news retrieval engine must handle both exact keyword lookups and abstract conceptual inquiries with equal precision.

Historically, search systems have relied on \textbf{sparse lexical matching algorithms} such as Term Frequency--Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) and the probabilistic Okapi BM25. In these classical approaches, documents and queries are represented as sparse vectors over a fixed vocabulary, and an inverted index structure enables near-instantaneous keyword lookup.

However, purely lexical methods face fundamental linguistic limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \textbf{The Vocabulary Mismatch Problem:} A user querying \textit{``automobile shares slump''} will fail to match an article titled \textit{``car stock market drops''} because the authors used different synonyms for the same concept.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Semantic Context:} Lexical models operate under the \textit{bag-of-words} assumption, treating words as isolated independent tokens and ignoring synonyms, polysemy (words with multiple meanings), and contextual relationships.
    \item \textbf{Term Saturation Sensitivity:} Simple frequency counters can be distorted by keyword repetition, requiring careful term frequency damping and document length normalization.
\end{enumerate}

Recent advances in deep learning introduced \textbf{dense semantic retrieval} through Sentence Transformers (such as Sentence-BERT). These models encode passages and queries into high-dimensional continuous vector embeddings (e.g., 384 dimensions) where geometric proximity corresponds to semantic relatedness. While dense models excel at understanding context and synonyms, they are computationally heavier and can sometimes overlook exact named entities or rare technical terms.

\subsection{Project Objectives}
The primary objectives of this project are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Combine sparse lexical retrieval (Okapi BM25) and dense semantic retrieval (Sentence Transformers) using \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}.
    \item Eliminate the vocabulary mismatch problem using automated \textbf{WordNet Query Expansion}.
    \item Provide instant query-focused extractive summaries as \textbf{Featured Snippets}.
    \item Offer explainability through an interactive \textbf{3D PCA Semantic Map}.
    \item Enable live breaking news categorization and indexing using a trained \textbf{Logistic Regression} model.
    \item Measure retrieval quality using standard IR metrics: Precision@10, Recall, Mean Average Precision (MAP), and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG@10).
\end{itemize}

\section{About Dataset}

The empirical evaluations in this report are conducted on the benchmark \textbf{BBC News Archive Dataset}, widely used in natural language processing and information retrieval research (Greene and Cunningham, 2006). The corpus comprises \textbf{2,225 full-length news articles} published between 2004 and 2005.

The articles are categorized into five distinct topical domains:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Business (510 articles, 22.9\%):} Financial markets, corporate mergers, economic policies, international trade, inflation, and interest rate announcements.
    \item \textbf{Sport (511 articles, 23.0\%):} Athletic competitions, match results, tournament updates, football championships, rugby, athletics, and player transfers.
    \item \textbf{Politics (417 articles, 18.7\%):} Government policy, parliamentary debates, election campaigns, legislation, political scandals, and diplomatic summits.
    \item \textbf{Tech (401 articles, 18.0\%):} Computer hardware, consumer electronics, internet security, software developments, artificial intelligence, and digital privacy.
    \item \textbf{Entertainment (386 articles, 17.4\%):} Cinema, music, television, celebrity news, music charts, festival releases, and awards (e.g., the Oscars).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dual-Stream Preprocessing Architecture}
A critical design decision in our system is that lexical search and neural transformers require fundamentally different text preparation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Lexical Preprocessing (for BM25 and TF-IDF):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Text is converted to lowercase and all punctuation is stripped.
        \item Non-informative grammatical stop words (e.g., \textit{``the''}, \textit{``is''}, \textit{``at''}) are removed using NLTK's English stopword corpus.
        \item Words are reduced to their root forms using the \textbf{Porter Stemmer} (e.g., \textit{``investments''} becomes \textit{``invest''}, \textit{``computing''} becomes \textit{``comput''}).
        \item An \textbf{Inverted Index} is constructed, mapping each unique stemmed term to the list of documents containing that term along with its frequency, enabling rapid $\mathcal{O}(1)$ keyword lookup.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Preprocessing (for Sentence Transformers):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In contrast, the Sentence Transformer is provided with \textbf{raw, grammatically intact sentences}.
        \item Deep neural networks rely on multi-head self-attention mechanisms where capitalization, punctuation, and grammatical stop words provide essential syntactic cues. Removing them degrades the quality of the generated embeddings.
        \item Articles are truncated to a 300-word context window to capture the journalistic lead while conforming to the transformer's token input constraints.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Retrieval Models}
We implement and benchmark four distinct ranking strategies:

\subsubsection{1. TF-IDF Vector Space Model (Baseline)}
The Term Frequency--Inverse Document Frequency model represents each document and query as sparse vectors in a high-dimensional vocabulary space. Relevance between query vector $\mathbf{q}$ and document vector $\mathbf{d}$ is computed using Cosine Similarity:
\begin{equation}
\text{Sim}_{\text{TF-IDF}}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\| \|\mathbf{d}\|}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
    \item $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}$ is the dot product of the query and document TF-IDF vectors,
    \item $\|\mathbf{q}\|$ and $\|\mathbf{d}\|$ are the Euclidean norms (magnitudes) of the query and document vectors respectively.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{2. Okapi BM25 Probabilistic Model}
Okapi BM25 improves on TF-IDF by introducing non-linear term saturation and document length normalization:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}_{\text{BM25}}(D, Q) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{IDF}(q_i) \cdot \frac{f(q_i, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q_i, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
    \item $Q = \{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\}$ is the set of query terms,
    \item $D$ is the candidate document being scored,
    \item $f(q_i, D)$ is the frequency of query term $q_i$ in document $D$,
    \item $|D|$ is the length of document $D$ measured in words,
    \item $\text{avgdl}$ is the average document length across the entire corpus,
    \item $\text{IDF}(q_i)$ is the Inverse Document Frequency of term $q_i$, measuring how rare or common the term is across all documents,
    \item $k_1 = 1.5$ is the term saturation parameter (it caps the marginal score benefit of repeating the same keyword),
    \item $b = 0.75$ is the length normalization parameter (it penalizes longer documents proportionally to how much they exceed the average length).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Sentence Transformers (Dense Semantic Retrieval)}
We use the pre-trained \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model, a 6-layer MiniLM transformer. It encodes text into a continuous 384-dimensional dense vector space $\mathbb{R}^{384}$. Relevance between query vector $\mathbf{u}$ and document vector $\mathbf{v}$ is evaluated using Cosine Similarity:
\begin{equation}
\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
    \item $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ is the dense embedding vector of the search query,
    \item $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ is the dense embedding vector of a candidate document,
    \item A score close to $1.0$ indicates high semantic similarity; a score close to $0$ indicates low similarity.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4. Hybrid Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}
A key challenge in combining BM25 and Sentence Transformers is that BM25 outputs unbounded positive scores ($[0, \infty)$) while Cosine Similarity outputs normalized scores ($[-1, 1]$). Directly adding these raw scores is mathematically unsound because BM25 would dominate.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) solves this by relying entirely on the \textbf{ordinal rank position} of each document, ignoring raw scores:
\begin{equation}
\text{RRF\_Score}(d) = \sum_{m \in M} \frac{1}{k + r_m(d)}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
    \item $d$ is a candidate document being scored,
    \item $M = \{\text{BM25}, \text{Dense}\}$ is the set of ranking models being fused,
    \item $r_m(d)$ is the 1-based rank position of document $d$ in the ranked list produced by model $m$ (e.g., if BM25 ranks a document 3rd, then $r_{\text{BM25}}(d) = 3$),
    \item $k = 60$ is the standard smoothing constant (Cormack et al., 2009). It dampens the dominance of rank~1 documents from any single model and ensures that documents consistently ranked highly across \textit{both} models receive the highest combined score.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why RRF works so well:} Because $\frac{1}{60 + r}$ is a harmonic decay function, only documents that appear near the top of \textit{both} the BM25 list and the Sentence Transformer list receive a large combined score. A document that ranks 1st in BM25 but 500th in Semantic receives a much lower RRF score than a document ranked 3rd in both.

\subsection{Key Features Beyond Basic Search}
To enhance practical search utility, the system integrates four specialized features:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Automated WordNet Query Expansion:} The system queries Princeton's WordNet lexical database to automatically identify synonyms for each content term in the query. Up to 2 high-confidence synonyms per term are appended to the query string behind the scenes, bridging vocabulary gaps before search evaluation begins.
    \item \textbf{Query-Focused Extractive Summarization (Featured Snippets):} When an article is retrieved, the system tokenizes it into individual sentences using NLTK's sentence tokenizer. Each sentence is scored based on its stemmed token overlap with the query. The top 1--2 sentences with the highest overlap are extracted and presented as an instant answer snippet (100\% factual with zero hallucination risk, since only exact quotes are displayed).
    \item \textbf{3D Semantic Map (Explainable AI):} Using \textbf{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}, the 384-dimensional dense document vectors are compressed into 3D coordinates ($X, Y, Z$). All documents and the user query are plotted in an interactive Plotly scatter plot, allowing users to visually verify that semantically related articles (e.g., all ``business'' articles) cluster together in vector space.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time ML News Categorization:} The system trains a \textbf{Logistic Regression} classifier on the TF-IDF feature matrix (achieving 96.63\% validation accuracy on an 80/20 train-test split). Users can paste new articles into the interface; the model predicts the article's category and dynamically inserts it into both the lexical inverted index and dense vector store in under 0.5~seconds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate search performance using four standard Information Retrieval metrics:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Precision@10 (P@10):} The proportion of the top 10 retrieved articles that belong to the expected topic category:
    \begin{equation}
    P@10 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{10} \text{rel}_i}{10}
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{rel}_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary indicator that equals $1$ if the document at rank position $i$ belongs to the expected category, and $0$ otherwise.

    \item \textbf{Recall:} The fraction of all relevant documents in the corpus that were successfully retrieved within the top $K$ results:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Recall} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K} \text{rel}_i}{|R|}
    \end{equation}
    where $|R|$ is the total number of relevant documents for that category in the entire corpus. \textit{Note:} For $K=10$ and categories of approximately 400--510 articles, the theoretical maximum possible recall is $10/400 \approx 0.025$ (2.5\%).

    \item \textbf{Mean Average Precision (MAP):} Evaluates the quality of ranking by computing average precision across all relevant positions. The denominator is bounded by the retrieval window $K$ to avoid artificially low scores:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{AP@K} = \frac{1}{\min(K, |R|)} \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(k) \times \text{rel}_k
    \end{equation}
    where:
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
        \item $K$ is the number of documents retrieved (the retrieval window, e.g., 10),
        \item $|R|$ is the total number of relevant documents in the corpus for the queried category,
        \item $P(k) = \frac{\text{Number of relevant documents in top } k}{k}$ is the precision computed at rank position $k$,
        \item $\text{rel}_k \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary relevance of the document at rank $k$,
        \item $\min(K, |R|)$ bounds the denominator so that when $K < |R|$, the metric evaluates against the maximum achievable performance within the retrieval window rather than the entire category.
    \end{itemize}
    MAP is the mean of AP values across all evaluation queries: $\text{MAP} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \text{AP}(q)$.

    \item \textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG@10):} Measures ranking quality by applying a logarithmic discount to documents placed lower in the ranking, heavily rewarding models that place the most relevant documents at position~1:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{DCG@10} = \sum_{i=1}^{10} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \quad \text{nDCG@10} = \frac{\text{DCG@10}}{\text{IDCG@10}}
    \end{equation}
    where:
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, topsep=2pt]
        \item $\text{rel}_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the relevance of the document at rank position $i$,
        \item $\log_2(i + 1)$ is the logarithmic discount factor that penalizes relevant documents placed at lower positions,
        \item $\text{IDCG@10}$ (Ideal DCG) is the maximum possible DCG score, obtained by sorting all relevant documents at the top (i.e., a hypothetical perfect ranking).
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion of Findings}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Why BM25 Outperforms TF-IDF:} Okapi BM25 improves Precision@10 from 0.420 to 0.610 (+45.2\%) and MAP from 0.384 to 0.530 (+38.0\%). This gain is driven by two factors: (i) BM25's term saturation parameter ($k_1 = 1.5$) prevents documents that repeat keywords from disproportionately dominating, and (ii) the length normalization parameter ($b = 0.75$) penalizes longer articles so they do not automatically outrank shorter, more focused articles.

    \item \textbf{Why Sentence Transformers Outperform BM25:} Sentence Transformers achieve a MAP of 0.795 and an nDCG@10 of 0.884. By encoding queries and documents into continuous 384-dimensional vector space, the model recognizes conceptual synonyms (e.g., matching \textit{``economic downturn''} with \textit{``recession''}) that keyword-based models fundamentally cannot detect.

    \item \textbf{Why Hybrid RRF Achieves the Highest Scores:} The Hybrid RRF model achieves the best performance across every metric: \textbf{0.940 Precision@10}, \textbf{0.912 MAP}, and \textbf{0.965 nDCG@10}. By fusing both BM25 and Semantic rankings through ordinal rank positions, RRF ensures that documents supported by both exact keyword evidence and semantic meaning consensus are placed at the very top of the final ranking.

    \item \textbf{Note on Recall Values:} While Precision, MAP, and nDCG are exceptionally high, Recall values appear numerically modest ($\approx 0.025$). This is mathematically expected: because each category contains approximately 400--510 relevant articles, retrieving only $K=10$ documents imposes a theoretical recall ceiling of $10 / 400 = 0.025$ (2.5\%). This is not a system limitation---it reflects the inherent constraint of evaluating at a small retrieval depth $K$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

In this project, we designed, implemented, and evaluated an advanced \textbf{Hybrid Information Retrieval System} for the BBC News Archive.

The experimental results validate that:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Purely lexical search (TF-IDF and BM25) is computationally efficient but fundamentally limited by the vocabulary mismatch problem---it cannot match documents that express the same concept using different words.
    \item Purely semantic search (Sentence Transformers) understands context, intent, and synonyms, but can occasionally overlook exact named entities or rare technical keywords.
    \item \textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)} provides the optimal synthesis, achieving an exceptional \textbf{0.965 nDCG@10} and \textbf{0.912 MAP}, decisively outperforming all standalone search baselines.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, the integration of automated WordNet query expansion, query-focused extractive snippets, 3D semantic mapping, and real-time news categorization (Logistic Regression at 96.63\% accuracy) creates an accurate, explainable, and practical search engine.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Scope}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Compute Overhead:} Generating dense transformer embeddings on CPU requires noticeable initialization time. In a production deployment with millions of documents, specialized Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) vector databases (such as Faiss, Milvus, or Pinecone) would be necessary for sub-millisecond vector search.
    \item \textbf{Extractive vs. Generative Answers:} Our snippet generator is strictly extractive---it quotes exact sentences from articles to eliminate AI hallucinations. Future work could integrate lightweight generative cross-encoder re-rankers (such as MonoT5 or ColBERT) to synthesize concise multi-sentence answers when relevant information is spread across different paragraphs.
    \item \textbf{Proxy Relevance Labels:} Our evaluation uses category-based proxy relevance judgments (a document is deemed relevant if it belongs to the expected category). For more fine-grained evaluation, manually annotated relevance labels at the query-document pair level could be incorporated.
\end{itemize}
